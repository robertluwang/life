## A Good Card at Home, a Bad Card in China

I was looking at my CIBC Costco card on my desk in Montreal and realizing it was practically useless for my upcoming trip to China. For years it has been my default for everyday spending here in Canada. But the moment a transaction is processed in a foreign currency, CIBC tacks on a 2.5 percent foreign transaction fee. If I used it to book a 4000 CNY flight or pay for a hotel, I would be handing the bank a significant cut for doing absolutely nothing.

I had to figure out a cleaner way to pay for things. China operates almost entirely on QR codes now. Cash is legally accepted but functionally frustrating because many small vendors simply do not have change. You need to use WeChat Pay or Alipay, and as a visitor, you have to link a foreign credit card to these apps.

## Two Cards With No Foreign Exchange Fee

I decided to use two specific cards for the trip: an EQ Bank Mastercard and a Wise card. Neither charges foreign exchange fees. The EQ card pulls Canadian dollars from my account and converts them using the daily Mastercard rate. The Wise card lets me convert Canadian dollars to Chinese Yuan in the app beforehand and spends straight from that balance.

Binding the cards to WeChat was straightforward. I verified my identity using my Canadian passport and entered the EQ card number. But getting the card on the app is only half the battle. You have to navigate the fees the Chinese platforms impose on foreign cards.

## The 200 CNY Rule

The main rule to remember with WeChat and Alipay is the 200 CNY threshold. If a single transaction is 200 CNY or less, the platform waives the service fee. If the transaction goes even one cent over 200 CNY, WeChat takes a 3 percent cut of the entire amount. For everyday things like convenience stores, a quick taxi ride, or a coffee, you stay well under the limit and pay exactly what the item costs. For a dinner that comes out to 350 CNY, the math changes. The easiest way around this is to just ask the waiter to split the bill into two smaller transactions. You scan your phone twice, keep both charges under 200, and the 3 percent fee disappears.

Big ticket items like hotels and flights require a different approach. You cannot split a hotel bill into twenty small QR code scans. You have to pay directly through the booking platform.

## Hotels Need a Different Route

I tested this by booking a hotel in China through the domestic Ctrip app. The total came to 3288 CNY. The domestic app usually has better prices than the international version, Trip.com, because it includes local subsidies. But Ctrip has aggressive fraud prevention for foreign cards. If you just go into your account settings and try to add a new Canadian Mastercard, the system rejects it. They do not allow empty card binding anymore.

I found a way around the block. I searched for a random, cheap hotel on Ctrip that offered free cancellation. I booked it, selected pay now, and entered the EQ card details at the checkout screen. Because it was tied to a real transaction, the system let the payment go through. The moment the booking was confirmed, I cancelled it. The refund was processed instantly, and the hold on my EQ account was released. More importantly, my EQ card was now saved and verified in the Ctrip system.

I then went back to my actual 3288 CNY hotel and booked it using the newly saved EQ card. I made sure to select the pay now option rather than paying at the hotel. This is a critical detail. Most physical point of sale machines at independent Chinese hotels cannot process foreign Visa or Mastercard plastics. If you show up and your physical card is declined, the front desk will ask you to scan their WeChat QR code to pay the bill. Because a hotel bill is well over 200 CNY, you immediately get hit with the 3 percent WeChat fee. By paying online through Ctrip in advance, the transaction goes through Ctrip's international gateway. They do not charge a fee for hotel bookings, and my EQ card handles the currency conversion at zero markup. If my plans change, the cancellation policy ensures a full refund minus a 349 CNY penalty only if I fail to show up or cancel too late.

## The Currency Trap on International Sites

You also have to watch out for currency traps on international platforms. If you use Trip.com, Booking, or Expedia, the checkout page will often detect a Canadian card and ask if you want to pay in Canadian dollars. You must always decline this. It is a system called dynamic currency conversion. The platform applies a terrible exchange rate that usually includes a hidden markup of up to 5 percent. I always manually set the currency to CNY and let the EQ or Wise card handle the math.

## Trains Are the Easy Part

Trains are much simpler. China's high speed rail network is run through the official 12306 system. The app has an English version and accepts foreign credit cards without much friction. When you buy a ticket, it is linked directly to the passport number you used to register. There are no paper tickets to print. You just walk up to the manual inspection lane at the station, hand the staff your passport, and walk through to the platform.
