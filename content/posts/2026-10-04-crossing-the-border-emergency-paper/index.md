Before pulling out of the driveway in Montreal for the drive down to Philadelphia, I printed a single sheet of paper and folded it into the glovebox. We were heading south for an extended road trip to visit art museums, but driving the interstates put me on edge in a way driving at home does not. Once you cross the border at Champlain, the safety net of familiar local services disappears. You are suddenly dealing with state troopers, separate toll road authorities, and a completely different healthcare system. I wanted the worst-case scenarios documented in hard copy, because when a tire blows out at highway speeds or a medical crisis hits, nobody has the mental bandwidth to start searching for contact information on a phone.

## The sheet itself

This is what is on it, in roughly the order I would need it:

- Breakdown, New York State Thruway: 1-800-842-2233
- Breakdown, New Jersey Turnpike: 833-875-4243
- Breakdown, Pennsylvania Turnpike: 1-800-332-3880, or *11
- Road conditions in all three states: 511
- Medical, Sun Life assistance in the United States: 1-800-511-4610
- Medical, CIBC Allianz assistance: 1-800-281-9109
- Lost cards, CIBC: 1-800-663-4575
- Lost cards, EQ Bank: 1-844-437-2265
- Lost cards, Wise: +1-888-908-3833
- Canadian consular help, Emergency Watch and Response Centre: +1 613-996-8885 (collect), or 1-888-949-9993 toll-free from the United States

## If the car breaks down

The most common threat on a long road trip is a mechanical failure. We do not maintain an active CAA membership, so a breakdown on the interstate required a different approach. We drove south on toll-free roads, but the drive home ran up the big toll corridors, the New York State Thruway, the New Jersey Turnpike, and the Pennsylvania Turnpike, and we spent time on ordinary interstates in all three states either way. The toll systems operate under strict safety rules. You cannot just call an independent local garage to tow the vehicle off the shoulder. They only allow their own officially contracted services on the road. So if the engine fails, the plan is plain: call the dispatch number for the state we are in, wait for the authorized tow, and pay the driver directly with a bank card.

For minor roadside issues off the main toll roads, we rely on the Honk app. It functions like a ride-hailing service for tow trucks and jump-starts, without requiring an annual membership fee. And before setting out each day, dialing 511 on a cell phone gives live updates on weather delays and active road closures in whichever state you are calling from.

## If one of us gets sick or hurt

A dead battery is an inconvenience. A car crash or a sudden illness is a different order of problem, and it needs the insurance calls made in the right order. We carry overlapping travel medical coverage through my employer's Sun Life group benefits and the CIBC Aeroplan Visa. The rule on the sheet is simple. Securing the scene and calling 911 take absolute priority. Nobody delays an ambulance to keep an insurer happy. But the assistance company has to be contacted before treatment where that is possible, and in an emergency admission within 24 hours, and before any surgery. If I am incapacitated, my partner calls Sun Life or CIBC Allianz on my behalf, or I do the same for her. The insurance companies then take over the communication with the hospital administration to arrange direct billing where they can, because paying an American emergency room bill out of pocket and sorting it out later is not a realistic plan. If the physical Sun Life card is somehow lost during the chaos of an accident, the phone operator can locate the group policy details using just a name, date of birth, and my employer's name.

## If there is a crash

If there is a collision on the highway, getting medical help is the primary objective, but documenting the scene is the necessary second step. Do not drive away from a crash involving damage or injuries without exchanging information and reporting it. If a state trooper or local officer responds, ask for the incident report, or at least the report number, before leaving. Take clear photos of all involved vehicles, the surrounding road conditions, license plates, and the other driver's insurance card. Both Sun Life and CIBC will ask for an official police report where one exists, and for whatever documentation there is where one does not, because minor crashes with no injuries do not always get a police response. If a severe crisis occurs, such as a major medical transfer back to a hospital in Montreal or serious legal complications across the border, the backup plan is to call the 24/7 Canadian Emergency Watch and Response Centre at +1 613-996-8885.

## If the wallet disappears

The final disaster scenario mapped out on the printout is a lost or stolen wallet. Carrying passports, a Quebec driver's license, the EQ Bank card, and the CIBC cards in one place means losing everything at once leaves you stranded. The immediate reaction to a missing wallet should not be making frantic phone calls. The first move is opening the banking applications on a phone to instantly lock and freeze the EQ Bank, Wise, and CIBC cards. This stops unauthorized transactions immediately. But a freeze is a pause, not a solution. A frozen card is frozen in Apple Wallet too, so there is no keeping the digital version running while the physical one is locked. The freeze buys time while you retrace your steps and check the hotel room one more time. If the phone is dead or the applications fail to load, the 24-hour reporting lines on the sheet are the next step.

Once the wallet is truly gone, the cards have to be reported lost, not just frozen, and that step is what keeps the trip alive. Reporting a CIBC card lost in the app cancels the old number and pushes a replacement digital card into Apple Wallet right away, so essential purchases like gas, food, and hotel charges can still go through while the new plastic is in the mail. The critical administrative step after that is a police report where you are. In a city like Philadelphia, 311 is the city services line, and it will point you to the police district for the area where the wallet went missing; the district takes the report, and you keep the report number. That record does two important jobs. It supports you with the banks if any fraudulent charges were not authorized, and it explains your situation to the Canadian border guards when you pull up to the primary inspection booth without a physical provincial driver's license. It does not replace the license, and it will not shorten the conversation, but it turns a story into something documented. If the passports were inside the missing wallet, the only route home involves calling the Canadian consular line on the sheet to arrange for emergency travel documents.

We put the car in gear and pulled onto Highway 15 South. The paper sat quietly in the dark of the glovebox. You write down the worst possibilities, organize the phone numbers, and then you focus on the road ahead.
