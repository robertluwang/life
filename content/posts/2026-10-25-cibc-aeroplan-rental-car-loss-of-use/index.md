After I published the post about driving our own car down to Philadelphia, one question kept coming back in conversation. That post was about a Canadian policy following a Canadian car into the United States. Renting a car after you land is a different problem, and it starts at a counter, usually when you are tired, with an agent explaining that the damage waiver costs almost as much per day as the car itself.

The part of that pitch that works on me is loss of use. If you dent the rental car, the repair bill is easy to picture. Loss of use is the second bill, the money the rental company says it lost because that car sat in a body shop instead of going out with the next customer. A lot of card coverage sounds fine until you ask about that second bill, so I read the actual certificate for the CIBC Aeroplan Visa Infinite card, the version amended on July 1, 2025, underwritten by Belair Insurance Company Inc. Loss of use is in there, in plain words.

## What loss of use actually means in the certificate

The certificate defines loss of use as the amount paid to a rental agency to compensate it when a rental car is unavailable for rental while undergoing repairs for damage incurred during the coverage period. That is the whole concept, no mystery. The car cannot be rented, the agency wants to be paid for the downtime, and this definition is the hook the coverage hangs on.

Section 5 then says this insurance covers theft, loss of, or damage to the rental car up to its actual cash value, and valid rental agency loss of use charges. That word valid is doing real work. The insurer can ask the agency to show the charge is genuine, with a repair bill and a repair timeline, instead of paying whatever number appears on a counter invoice. That is still far better than a certificate that excludes loss of use outright, which is the gap I had assumed was normal.

## Primary coverage, and no deductible

Two more lines matter at the counter. There is no deductible for this coverage. And the insurance is primary insurance, except for losses the rental agency or its insurer has waived or assumed, and except where local government insurance legislation says otherwise.

Primary is the word to underline. With secondary coverage, you claim against your own auto policy first, your insurer pays, your record takes the hit, and the card cleans up what is left. With primary coverage, the card insurer pays first for the rental car itself, and your personal policy stays out of that part of the claim. For a Canadian renting in the United States, that separation is the main reason to bother with card coverage at all.

## The activation rules are strict, and they are all on you

None of this switches on by holding the card in your wallet. The conditions are mechanical, and every one of them is the renter job to get right before driving away.

The full cost of the rental, including applicable taxes, has to be charged to the card, and you have to start and finish the rental transaction on the same card. If you pay with Aeroplan points or another CIBC travel benefit, a partial points payment still works, as long as the entire remaining balance goes on the card. A free rental day combined with paid days works the same way, with the paid part and the taxes on the card.

You also have to decline the rental agency Collision Damage Waiver, or the Loss Damage Waiver as it is called in the United States, on the rental agreement. Only the cardholder can rent the car and make that decline. If someone else does the paperwork and declines for you, coverage is void. Only one rental car is covered at a time, so if you rent a second car in the same period, only the first one carries this insurance.

These are small clerical things, and that is why they worry me. Nobody loses this coverage in an argument about risk. They lose it because a spouse signed at the counter, or part of the bill went on a different card at return, or the agent added the waiver back and nobody read the final agreement.

## The 48 day limit voids everything, not just the extra days

The summary page lists a rental period of up to 48 days, and the detailed section is blunter than the summary. If the length of time you rent the same vehicle, or vehicles back to back, exceeds 48 consecutive days, no coverage is provided at all, not for the first 48 days and not for anything after. Signing a fresh agreement with the same agency, or a different one, does not reset the clock. A full calendar day with no rental has to pass before the count breaks.

For a holiday rental of a week or two, this never comes up. For a long stay, it is a hard wall at day 49 that takes the whole rental back to uninsured. I would put a note in my phone on day 40 rather than trust my memory.

## The 85,000 dollar limit is about MSRP, not the rental price

The other ceiling is the car itself. Coverage applies to a car, a sport utility vehicle, or a mini-van with a Manufacturer Suggested Retail Price of 85,000 Canadian dollars or less in its model year, excluding taxes, at the place the agreement is signed or the car is picked up. A vehicle over that line is an excluded vehicle, and there is no partial coverage up to the limit.

MSRP trips people up because it has nothing to do with what the car is worth on the lot today, or what you paid per day. A premium trim that listed above 85,000 dollars when new stays above the line even after two years of fleet wear. CIBC disclosure puts the Infinite Privilege version at 100,000 dollars for the same coverage, so the limit is a product tier decision, not a safety rule. If the counter offers a free upgrade, that is the moment to think. A larger car can quietly move you out of coverage, and the certificate warns that agencies do not classify vehicles, especially mini-vans, the same way the insurer does. When the class is borderline, call the certificate number before you decline the waiver.

## What this coverage does not do

The exclusions section is where I slowed down, because it lines up with the warning in my earlier post. This card coverage does not pay third party liability. It does not pay personal injury or damage to property, except for the rental car itself and its equipment. In plain terms, if you injure someone or hit another car in the United States, this benefit does not answer that claim. You need liability cover from somewhere else, your own auto policy if it extends to rentals, or a separate arrangement, and you need to know which one it is before the trip. The certificate says this directly and tells you to check with your personal automobile insurer and the rental agency about adequate liability, personal injury, and property damage coverage.

Diminished value is also excluded, by name. That is the amount the agency says the car resale value dropped because it now has an accident history, even after a proper repair. The card will pay to fix the car and pay valid loss of use while it is being fixed, and then stop. If an agency assesses diminished value anyway, that argument lands on you, and the disclosure material warns that some agencies assess damages above actual cash value, with the difference also left to you.

Your belongings inside the car are not covered here either. A stolen laptop or bag is a home or tenant insurance conversation, the same as it was in my own car on the Philadelphia drive.

## The excluded vehicle list is longer than the counter poster

Beyond the price ceiling, the certificate excludes vans and cargo vans other than mini-vans, trucks and pick-up trucks, limousines, off-road vehicles, motorcycles and motor bikes, trailers, campers, and recreational vehicles. Handmade and low production cars are out, antique cars are out, and tax-free lease cars are out. A vehicle towing anything is out.

One definition caught my eye. A rental agency under this certificate means a licensed agency and carsharing programs, but it does not include car dealerships, and it does not include peer-to-peer carsharing companies that rent cars through apps. Renting a neighbour car through an app may feel identical to renting from a counter, and for this insurance it is not a rental at all.

There are also the conduct exclusions you would expect. Driving intoxicated, a dishonest or criminal act, racing, intentional damage, and using the wrong fuel are not covered. Operating the car against the rental agreement is generally excluded too, with three useful exceptions written back in: insured drivers may drive it, it may be driven on publicly maintained gravel roads, and it may cross provincial and state boundaries in Canada and the United States, and between the two countries. That last exception makes the card usable for the trips I actually take.

## If something happens, the clock is short

The claim instructions are not generous with time. You have to call within 48 hours of the theft, loss, or damage. From Canada and the United States the number is 1 866 363 3338, and from anywhere else you call collect to 905 403 3338. Global Excel handles assistance and claims for Belair under this certificate.

The claim itself has to be submitted with as much documentation as possible within 45 days, and all documentation has to be in within 90 days. The list includes card statements, both sides of the rental agreement, the damage report, the itemized repair bill, and the receipt for paid repairs. So photograph the car at pickup and at return, keep every page of the agreement, and be there for the final inspection if you can. Your responsibility does not end when you drop the keys in a box. It ends when their inspection report is done.

And do not sign a blank sales draft to cover damage and loss of use charges, or one with an estimated repair cost on it. Signing that paper can leave you responsible for numbers nobody has proven yet.

## How I would use this at the counter

For an ordinary passenger car or SUV, under the MSRP limit, on a rental well under 48 days, on a trip in Canada or the United States, this is strong coverage. I would charge the full rental to the card, decline the CDW or LDW myself and in my own name, check that the decline actually shows on the final agreement, and photograph the car on both ends of the rental. I would keep the certificate PDF on my phone, with the claim number saved beside it, the same way I now keep a paper insurance slip in the glovebox of my own car.

I would still do one thing the card cannot do for me. I would confirm my liability cover for that rental before leaving home, because the largest risk in an American accident was never the rental car bodywork. It was, and remains, the claim from the other side.

I am a policyholder reading paperwork, not an insurance broker, and certificates change. This one says it may be cancelled, changed, or modified at any time, and it replaced every earlier version in July 2025. Download the current certificate for your own card before a trip and read the car rental section with your dates and vehicle class in front of you. It takes about ten minutes.
