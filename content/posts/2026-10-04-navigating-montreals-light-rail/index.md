Living in Montreal means watching construction projects drag on for a decade. The REM light rail was one of them. The South Shore branch has been running since 2023, and the West Island branch opened this past May, adding four stations out to Anse-à-l'Orme. Now that the trains actually reach both ends of the city, I have had to figure out how the system integrates with the rest of it. The physical train is modern, but dealing with the ticketing system feels like stepping back in time to navigate municipal bureaucracy.
The transit authority overhauled the entire fare structure to prepare for the REM. They threw out the old rules and divided the region into strict geographic zones. Zone A covers the entire island of Montreal. Zone B covers Laval and the Longueuil agglomeration on the South Shore. The map does not stop there. Zones C and D continue outward into the farther suburbs, and every line on it has a fare attached. Distance means absolutely nothing here. I can get on the train at Gare Centrale downtown and ride all the way out to Anse-à-l'Orme. It takes a long time, but it stays strictly within the boundaries of Zone A, so I pay the base fare of $3.75. But if I stand on Nun's Island and take the train one single stop across the Champlain Bridge to Brossard, I have crossed an invisible political line into Zone B. That one stop costs $5 and requires a cross-zone ticket.
You cannot just use a standard bus ticket to open the gates at a REM station. The system requires an "All modes" fare. When you buy this specific ticket, it gives you a 120-minute window to transfer freely between the light rail, the metro, and the local buses. If you try to tap a bus-only pass on the REM turnstile, a red light flashes and the gates stay shut.
The biggest point of friction in the entire network is the OPUS card. Most residents carry a standard blue plastic OPUS card in their wallets. These cards run on an outdated system, and they carry a limitation that makes no sense to a normal passenger. The card can hold a monthly cross-zone pass. A monthly All Modes AB pass costs $170, against $110 for Zone A alone. But the same card cannot store a single, two-trip, or ten-trip ticket for multiple zones. Those fares can only live on a designated All Modes AB OPUS card, or on a disposable cardboard smart ticket called L'Occasionnelle. If you live in the city and decide to take the REM to the Quartier DIX30 shopping center on the South Shore for the afternoon, you cannot load the $9.75 two-trip AB ticket onto your standard card. The station ticket machine will reject the transaction. You end up carrying your permanent plastic card and your temporary cardboard card, trying to remember which one holds the right fare.
There is a permanent fix, and it says a lot about the system. At a metropolitan ticket office, you can pick up the designated AB card, and it is free if you show a registered OPUS card in your name. The official solution to a card that cannot hold your fare is a second card in the same wallet.
There is exactly one piece of technology that makes using the REM tolerable, and it is the official Chrono app. Set your expectations first. Paying by tapping a bank card at the turnstile still does not exist here. Tapping a phone exists only in a narrow form. Android users can now store a virtual fare in Chrono and tap their phone at the gate, but that option has not arrived on the iPhone, so for me the phone is still a ticket machine rather than a ticket. You open Chrono, select the fare you need, and pay with your credit card. Then the app prompts you to hold your OPUS card flat against the back of your phone. The phone uses its NFC antenna to burn the ticket data directly onto the chip inside the plastic card. I handle this in my kitchen while drinking coffee before I leave the house. For a DIX30 trip, the card I hold against the phone is the designated AB card. Chrono cannot put an AB trip onto a standard card either. The app removes the queue, and that is all it removes. It still beats standing in the concourse at Gare Centrale, waiting behind crowds of people who are staring at the complex zone map on the ticket machine screens.
The actual ride on the REM is straightforward. The trains are fully automated. There is no driver cabin, so you can stand right at the front glass and watch the tracks ahead. The acceleration is quick. On the West Island branch the trains run every 15 minutes through the day, so a missed train is an annoyance rather than a disaster. The ride is louder and rougher than the rubber-tired metro trains, but it bypasses the bridge traffic entirely. When you reach your destination, you just walk out through the open gates. You do not tap your card to exit. Just make sure you paid for the correct zone. Transit inspectors board the trains randomly with handheld scanners. If they catch you riding in Brossard with a Zone A ticket on your card, the fine is heavy and they do not care about your excuses.
My routine now is simple. I work out the zone before I leave the house, load the right card at the kitchen table, and avoid the physical ticket machines whenever possible. The frustration has not disappeared. It has moved into my wallet, in the form of a second OPUS card, and into a small routine I run before I go anywhere near a station.
