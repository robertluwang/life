Before packing the trunk for the drive down to Philadelphia, most of us check the tire pressure, clean the windshield, and assume the glovebox paperwork will take care of the rest. We know a Canadian driver's licence works fine down south, and we know our cars are allowed on American highways. Once you cross that border line, though, the legal framework under your wheels changes.

A standard provincial automobile policy, written on Quebec's Q.P.F. 1 form or Ontario's O.A.P. 1, does follow your vehicle into the United States. You do not need to pull over at customs and buy a temporary border sticker. Your insurer back home treats a drive to Philadelphia, Boston, or Lake Placid as a normal part of owning a car in Canada. Being legally covered is rarely the problem. The problem starts when real money gets calculated.

## The liability limit is the whole game

Look at the liability limit printed on your slip. In Canada, one million dollars in third-party liability feels like a fortress. It is twenty times the legal minimum in Quebec and five times the minimum in Ontario, and it handles ordinary street mishaps without drama. Across the border, that number weakens fast. The American system resolves serious traffic injuries through civil lawsuits, and damage awards there routinely dwarf what Canadian courts hand out. On top of that, your one million is in Canadian dollars. In early October 2026, one Canadian dollar buys about seventy US cents, so a one-million-dollar Canadian policy behaves like roughly seven hundred thousand US dollars in an American courtroom. If a judgment against you comes in above that, your insurer hands over its maximum cheque and walks away. The remainder comes out of your savings and the equity in your home.

The fix is absurdly simple, and I only learned it because someone pointed it out. Before we left, I called my insurer and asked what raising our liability from one million to two would cost. About twenty dollars in total, the agent said. The change is temporary and mine to end. I could have let it stop when we got home, or leave it in place until our next renewal in 2027, and I left it running in case we drive south again in the next few months. Your price will depend on how much of your term is left, so treat my twenty dollars as an example, but get the quote before you decide it is too much trouble. Spending less than the cost of lunch to put another million between an American courtroom and your house is the easiest decision of the whole trip.

## Who actually drives the car

The second blind spot sits inside the household: who is really behind the wheel most days. Life shifts quietly. A family sells the second car, work moves home, and one vehicle ends up doing everything. The spouse who used to take the train now runs most of the errands, while the spouse listed as principal driver only takes the wheel on weekends. Canadian policies care a lot about that distinction between principal and occasional driver. If your household changed six months ago and the file never caught up, a major claim in the US is the worst possible moment to discover it. Cross-border adjusters read the declaration page line by line. Ten minutes on the phone aligning the policy with who actually puts the kilometres on the car removes any argument about misrepresentation when you need the coverage most.

## Theft, damage, and getting paid

Vehicle damage and theft carry their own surprises. If your car is broken into or stolen in an American downtown garage, your coverage for damage to the insured vehicle steps up, subject to the deductible you chose back home. Two details matter here. The things stolen from inside the car, a laptop, a bag, a camera, usually fall under your home or tenant insurance, not the auto policy. And unless your policy carries a waiver of depreciation endorsement, Q.E.F. 43 in Quebec and OPCF 43 in Ontario, a total loss pays out at actual cash value: what the car was worth as a used asset ten seconds before it vanished, minus your deductible.

Stolen vehicle claims also require patience. The local police take the report and enter the car in their systems, and most insurers then wait, often up to about thirty days, to see whether it turns up in an impound lot or an alley before settling. Nobody writes a cheque on day three.

While you wait, you still need to get around. The base Quebec contract already covers some travel costs after the theft of the whole vehicle, and an endorsement like Q.E.F. 20, sold on its own or inside a touring package, broadens that to travel expenses and rental costs after other covered losses, up to the limit written in the endorsement. Ontario readers will know the same idea as OPCF 20. Check the limit before you leave, because a rental on day twenty of a claim stops being cheap.

One more line on the declaration page deserves a look: the creditor. If the car is leased or financed, a creditor listed under an endorsement such as Q.E.F. 23a or 23b has rights the insurer has to respect, and settlement money can end up involving the lender. If you paid the loan off last spring and the bank is still listed, get it removed while nothing is wrong. Chasing a bank signature is a poor use of the weeks after a theft.

## Your body is a separate problem

Car insurance and human health get mixed up constantly, so it helps to separate two situations.

If you are hurt in a crash, your own injuries are handled back home. A Quebec resident injured in a traffic accident anywhere in the world is entitled to the same SAAQ compensation as if the accident had happened in Quebec, and for an accident the SAAQ also reimburses, in whole or in part, the hospital costs that the RAMQ does not. Ontario drivers fall back on their accident benefits, where the standard medical, rehabilitation, and attendant care limit is $65,000 for a non-catastrophic injury and $1,000,000 for a catastrophic one. Those are real numbers, and they are still easy for an American hospital to burn through, which is why the next paragraph matters.

If you get sick, or hurt yourself in a way that has nothing to do with the car, you are on travel insurance territory. Your provincial health card reimburses at home rates and comes nowhere near American prices. The RAMQ, for example, pays at most $100 a day for a hospital stay abroad and $50 a day for outpatient hospital care. Real coverage for that ER visit comes from employer group health benefits or the emergency travel insurance built into a premium credit card, and we travel on exactly that combination. Two habits make it work: keep the benefits card in your wallet, and call the assistance number on the back early, before non-urgent treatment where you can, so the insurer can arrange direct billing with the hospital. In a true emergency, call 911 first and let the paperwork follow.

## Before you turn the key

Print your insurance slip on real paper and put it in the glove compartment. The Canadian inter-provincial card is printed as applicable in Canada and the United States, and electronic proof is accepted in most states, so paper is a backup rather than a rule. It covers you when the phone battery dies, and it beats handing an unlocked phone to anyone at the roadside. While you are at it, confirm the two-million-dollar limit actually shows on your declaration page, and save your insurer's number and your travel assistance number in your phone.

One last note: I am a policyholder, not a broker, and every policy in the drawer at home has its own wording. The call I describe took me a few minutes and settled most of these questions for our car. Make the same call for yours. A long drive south is for open asphalt and roadside diners, not for reading fine print beside a tow truck in another country.
