## Two trips, two kinds of driving

Late September gave me a clean comparison on the same route. I drove from Montreal to Philadelphia with the navigation set to avoid tolls. A few days later I drove back north with tolls allowed, as far as Albany. Going south cost me zero dollars in tolls and took much longer. Coming north cost me cash at a New Jersey toll booth and a charge through a temporary account in New York, and the driving itself stayed easy the whole way. Anyone planning this drive gets to pick which currency they would rather spend, time or money, and the toll systems in New York and New Jersey decide how that money gets collected.

## The free way south

The first part of the toll-free drive fools you. Autoroute 15 south from Montreal becomes Interstate 87 at the border near Saint-Bernard-de-Lacolle, and the Adirondack Northway runs about 176 miles down to Albany without a single toll. It is wide, fast, and lined with forest. You can settle in, set a steady speed, and watch the Adirondacks roll past.

The character of the trip changes once you keep avoiding tolls south of Albany. The direct tolled route uses the New York Thruway, the New Jersey Turnpike, and one of the big Delaware River bridges. A toll-free setting pushes you away from those roads and onto smaller highways that climb and drop through hill country. Lanes narrow. Speed limits fall and rise with every small town. You slow down for main streets, speed up again on open stretches of farmland, and steer through curves where the tolled highway would have cut a straighter line.

That is the honest trade in the free direction. You get local views the interstate hides, farm stands and river towns and long looks at the hills, and you pay for them with time and attention. The drive south took much longer than the tolled drive north, and I felt the difference by the time I reached Philadelphia. A mountain road in daylight with a loose schedule is a pleasure. The same road at the end of a long driving day is work. The free route also crosses the Delaware on a smaller free bridge north of the city, so the last stretch into Philadelphia runs on local roads rather than a wide bridge approach.

Total tolls for the drive south: $0. Total cost, measured in hours and concentration: the highest of the whole trip.

## The toll way north

The return trip started with a free crossing, which surprises people. The Delaware River Port Authority bridges between New Jersey and Philadelphia, the Walt Whitman, the Benjamin Franklin, and the Betsy Ross, collect tolls in one direction only, westbound into Pennsylvania. A passenger car pays $6.00 heading into Philadelphia and nothing heading back into New Jersey. Southbound drivers without an E-ZPass transponder should also know that the bridge booths take cash or E-ZPass. Cards and tap payments stay in your pocket at those booths, so keep US bills in the car if you drive south on the tolled route.

In New Jersey I joined the Turnpike at Interchange 4 near Mount Laurel, heading north. The Turnpike still works on the old ticket system, and it works well when the machinery cooperates. You take a paper ticket when you enter, hand it over when you exit, and pay for the distance you drove. Lane choice matters here more than anywhere else on the trip. Lanes marked for E-ZPass expect a transponder on the windshield. A driver who uses one of those lanes without a transponder can end up with a violation notice and a $50 administrative fee added to the toll. A temporary account from New York carries no weight in New Jersey. Each state runs its own system, and the systems barely talk to each other.

At Interchange 4, the dispenser gave me no ticket. I had a lane to clear and traffic behind me, so I drove on. At Interchange 11 in Woodbridge I told the collector what had happened and paid $11.55 in cash. The receipt, printed on September 29 at 11:12, says it in plain capital letters: LOST TICKET - MAXIMUM TOLL. Without a ticket, the collector has no way to prove where you entered, so you pay the maximum charge for your exit. I kept the receipt and took a photo of it. That small slip is the record of the trip if you later ask the Turnpike Authority to look at the charge.

After I got home, I sent the New Jersey Turnpike Authority a refund request through njta.org, with the receipt details and the explanation that I entered at Interchange 4 and the machine gave me no ticket. The request asks for the difference between the maximum toll and the toll for the distance I actually drove. As of writing, the request is in and I have no answer yet, so $11.55 remains what that exit cost me. The practical lesson costs less than mine: slow down at the entry, wait until the ticket is actually in your hand, and only then pull away.

New York collects its money in a completely different way. The Thruway north to Albany has no toll booths at all. Gantries pass overhead, cameras read your plate, and traffic never slows down. Before the trip I downloaded the Tolls NY app and opened a Temporary Travel Account, the option the app lists for rentals, loaners, and future travel. I entered my Quebec license plate, added a credit card, and set the range of days I would be on the road.

That was the setup, anyway. As I write this, days after driving home, the temporary account still shows nothing. No Thruway tolls have appeared in the app yet. I expect them to turn up later. Until they do, the New York part of the trip sits in a strange limbo: the car passed under the gantries, the cameras did their work, and the bill exists nowhere I can see. It still beats waiting for a paper bill to reach a Montreal address, though the waiting itself has not gone away. It has moved into the app. The app itself tells you that a real E-ZPass remains the cheapest and easiest option for regular travel, and that advice holds for anyone who makes this drive often. For one trip, the temporary account was the whole setup, with no transponder on my windshield.

North of Albany, Interstate 87 leaves the Thruway and becomes the Northway again. The last leg home to the border is the same free highway I started on, which makes a tidy ending to the comparison. New York charges you on the southern half of the state and lets you ride free through the northern half, in both directions.

## Which way next time

The choice comes down to the day you have planned. The free road asks for time, daylight, and a driver who wants to see the country at town speed. It gives you scenery and a $0 toll total, and it takes much longer. The toll road asks for cash in New Jersey, a few minutes of setup in an app for New York, and a bit of attention at ticket machines and lane signs. It gives you wide lanes, steady speeds, and an arrival time you can predict.

On a loose September schedule, I would take the free road south again and enjoy it. For the drive home, even with the $11.55 lesson at Interchange 11, I would pay the tolls and take the wide highway north.
