My phone buzzed this afternoon with a number I actually recognize, though for all the wrong reasons. It was 1-800-959-8281 flashing on the screen. Anyone living in Canada who has ever filed a tax return knows that string of digits. It is the official, legitimate toll-free line for individual income tax enquiries at the Canada Revenue Agency.

Except I didn't pick up.

I just stared at it sitting there on the glass, vibrating against the kitchen counter while I was halfway through chopping vegetables. My thumb hovered over the screen for a second before I hit reject and sent it straight to voicemail.

You develop a certain kind of reflex living here. If a government agency actually needs you—truly, officially needs you—they have a very specific, paper-trail-heavy way of going about it. They send a thick envelope with a barcode in the corner, or they drop an automated alert into your inbox telling you there is new mail waiting in your secure online portal. They do not call your mobile on a random Tuesday afternoon to bark questions at you, and they certainly do not sound like a compressed audio stream coming out of a basement call center half a world away.

Still, seeing that specific number pop up gives you a split-second jolt of adrenaline. That is the entire point of caller ID spoofing. Scammers figured out years ago that nobody answers calls from random overseas digits or unrecognized local cells anymore. So they buy software that slaps a trustworthy coat of paint on their incoming signals. They slap the CRA's main switchboard number on their outgoing line just to get past your cognitive defenses. They want you to look down, see a familiar sequence, and let your guard down out of sheer bureaucratic panic.

After I hung up, I didn't guess or wonder. I walked over to the desk, opened up my browser, and logged directly into my CRA My Account.

The dashboard loaded with that familiar, understated government blue. I checked the mail section. Nothing. I checked the notices and the account balance. Zero flags, zero alerts, zero urgent messages demanding my immediate attention or threatening me with legal action.

The digital ecosystem of modern life is exhausting because you constantly have to treat your own communication tools like a hostile environment. You can't trust a text message about a missing package from Canada Post, you can't trust an email about your bank account getting locked, and now you can't even trust the caller ID when the tax man supposedly rings your line. Every single channel is contaminated with noise, pretending to be authority figures looking out for your interests.

The real CRA doesn't want to talk to you that badly on the phone, and they certainly aren't going to ask for your SIN or demand payment in iTunes gift cards or Bitcoin. If they want money, they send a bill through the mail or post an official assessment online where you can look at the math yourself.

I tossed the phone back on the counter and went back to cutting vegetables. The pot on the stove was starting to warm up. If the government actually wants to find me, they can leave a paper trail like everyone else. Until then, every ringing phone from an official number gets treated like what it usually is: a ghost on the line trying to steal an afternoon.
